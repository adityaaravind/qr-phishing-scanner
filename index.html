    How quishing works

    Attackers print a sticker, paste it over a real code — on a parking meter, restaurant table, or EV charger — and your phone does the rest. The red flags this scanner checks for:

    The lookalike domain

    paypa1.com or arnazon.com — one swapped character away from the real brand, close enough to pass a glance.

    The subdomain costume

    paypal.com.login-verify.example — the brand name is just a subdomain. The real destination is whatever comes after it.

    The shortener blindfold

    bit.ly/… shows you nothing. The destination could be anything, and it can be changed after the sticker is printed.

    The punycode mask

    xn--pple-43d.com renders in some apps as a familiar-looking name built from lookalike foreign characters.

    The @ trick

    In https://paypal.com@evil.example, everything before the @ is decoration. Your browser goes to the part after it.

    Not a link at all

    Codes can auto-join a Wi-Fi network, add a contact, or open a payment app with an address pre-filled. “It scanned fine” says nothing about what it did.

    Already scanned a bad one?

    Don't panic — opening a page is not the same as giving something away. Work through this in order:

    1. Close the tab and don't enter passwords, card numbers, or seed phrases on it. If you didn't type anything, you're usually fine.
    2. If you entered a password, change it on the real site or app right away — and anywhere else you reused it. Turn on passkeys or two-factor while you're there.
    3. If you entered card details, call the number on the back of the card, freeze it in your banking app, and watch for small test charges.
    4. If the code joined you to Wi-Fi, forget that network in your settings so your phone doesn't reconnect automatically.
    5. Tell the venue. A fake sticker on a parking meter or table is still catching the next person.
